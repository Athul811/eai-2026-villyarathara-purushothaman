## The hard parts

These are the parts that people find difficult. Read them before the
session. If you cannot follow one of them, make it your question.

- **A published contract is a promise that you cannot withdraw.** You can add an optional field. You cannot remove a field, and you cannot make an optional field mandatory. Consumers that you do not know depend on it.
- **Microservices with synchronous chains are one system with more failure points.** If four services each give 99.9 per cent availability, the chain gives 99.6 per cent. You made your availability the product of four numbers.
- **Compensation is not rollback.** No system reverses the completed steps for you. You write each reverse action, and you decide the order.

## Self-check

Answer these before the session. They are not graded, and I do not
collect them. They show you which of the hard parts you cannot yet
explain. **A question that you cannot answer is your question for the
session.**

1. Your API has an optional field. You want to make it mandatory. Why is this a breaking change?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Existing clients do not send the field. After the change, their requests fail. Only additive and optional changes are safe on a published contract.

   </details>

2. Four services call each other in a synchronous chain. Each service gives 99.9 per cent availability. What availability does the chain give?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   About 99.6 per cent, which is 0.999 to the power of four. That is about three hours each month. Each service that you add multiplies the risk.

   </details>

3. A saga completes payment, then reserves stock, then fails at the courier step. What must happen, and in what order?
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   You compensate in reverse order. First release the stock reservation. Then refund the payment. Reverse order matters, because a later step can depend on an earlier one.

   </details>

## What the assignment will require

**PA6 — contract-first API and a saga**

You write `openapi.yaml` for POST /checkout first. A linter checks it. Then you write the implementation.
POST /checkout accepts the canonical order from PA4.
I check the order of your commits. You must commit the specification before you commit the implementation.
The assignment assumes that you can do these things:
- Write an OpenAPI specification by hand.
- Define an error response, and not only a success response.
- Call four services in sequence.
- Add up money in decimal strings, without floating-point numbers.
- Write a reverse action for each step that has one.
- Compensate in reverse order after a failure.
